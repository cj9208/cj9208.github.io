# Reorg Conversation Strategy

## The Situation

You are in a slow, unclear reorg.

Your current team is strong. People from the incoming larger org are approaching team members directly. Your current tech lead said he was not approached. That is an important signal.

This means the company may value the people in the team, but may not want to preserve the team as a unit.

The risk is not only org change. The deeper risk is being moved into a setup where you are treated as execution capacity instead of as a technical force.

That is why the question is not only where you can go. The question is where you can still do meaningful technical work and remain visible for it.

## The Core Reading

This likely is not only a technical reorganization.

It may also be a control and reshaping exercise:

- keep the strong people
- dilute the old team structure
- build new reporting relationships directly
- redistribute capability into weaker areas

That does not automatically mean bad intent. But it does mean you should think politically and structurally, not just technically.

## Your Specific Concern

One possible landing place matches your interests better, but the team leader seems weak in system design thinking.

He appears nice, team-friendly, and approachable. That is useful. But when discussing his product, he does not seem to think deeply in terms of system design, and he explains pressure mainly through backlog and delivery load.

That creates a specific risk:

- nice manager
- weak technical framing
- backlog-heavy environment
- strong chance of becoming ticket-driven

This does not mean you should reject him immediately. It means you need to test whether he knows how to use a stronger engineer well.

## What You Should Pay Attention To

The most important question is not whether he is personally nice.

The real question is whether he can create room for technical ownership.

Watch for these signals.

Good signs:

- he can understand system-level risks once explained clearly
- he sees technical debt as delivery risk, not just engineer preference
- he is open to giving ownership of a subsystem or cross-cutting problem
- he wants strong engineers involved earlier in shaping solutions
- he can translate technical improvements into roadmap benefit

Warning signs:

- every issue gets reduced to backlog pressure
- success is framed mainly as ticket throughput
- system design concerns are acknowledged politely but never prioritized
- there is no mechanism for foundational work
- he wants senior engineers mainly as extra delivery capacity

The difference is simple:

- weak in tech but open to technical leadership from others: workable
- weak in tech and purely backlog-driven: dangerous

## Your Goal In The Conversation

Do not go into the discussion trying to prove that you are better technically.

That will create defensiveness and usually fails.

Your real goal is to make one thing clear:

You are most valuable when you own higher-leverage technical problems, not when you are treated as a developer who only burns down tickets.

So the meeting should do three things:

- show how you think
- show the kind of value you create
- test whether he has room for that kind of value in his team

## The Story You Should Tell

The story is not:

"I do not want to do tickets."

The story is:

"I am happy to deliver, but my strongest contribution is on ambiguous, system-level, cross-cutting problems that improve delivery for more than one feature."

That framing is important because it sounds constructive, team-oriented, and high leverage.

It avoids sounding ego-driven.

## The Strategy For The Talk

### 1. Start From Delivery Outcomes

Start from the product or system, not from your personal preference.

Use language like:

> I have been thinking about this area, and I see a few design points that may affect delivery speed and maintainability over time.

This helps him hear you as someone trying to help the team, not someone asking for special treatment.

### 2. Bring One Or Two Concrete System Design Topics

Do not stay abstract.

Pick one or two issues in his current product where system design matters, for example:

- unclear service boundaries
- tight coupling between components
- repeated logic across flows
- scaling bottlenecks
- fragile integration points
- operational complexity that will slow future features

The goal is not to criticize his design. The goal is to demonstrate how you think.

### 3. Translate Technical Points Into Delivery Language

He may not respond best to architecture vocabulary alone.

Translate your points into outcomes he can use:

- slower feature delivery later
- repeated bugs and rework
- higher support or debugging cost
- difficulty onboarding new engineers
- reduced ability to evolve the system cleanly

This is how you make your technical value legible to a less technical manager.

### 4. Position Yourself As A Multiplier

Make it clear that your value is leverage.

Use lines like:

> I am happy to deliver features, but I usually add the most value when I can help solve structural problems behind repeated delivery pressure.

> I am strongest when I can shape technical direction in an area that affects multiple streams of work.

> I think I can help the team move faster over time, not just close individual items.

### 5. Ask About Ownership, Not Freedom

Do not ask vaguely for more strategic work.

Ask about concrete ownership.

For example:

> If I joined your team, would there be room for me to take ownership of problems like this?

> How do you usually make space for foundational or system-level work when backlog pressure is high?

> What kind of technical ownership could someone like me take on beyond normal feature execution?

This forces the conversation into something practical.

### 6. Test Whether His Model Goes Beyond Backlog

This is the key diagnostic.

You want to know whether he:

- understands the value once it is explained
- has a way to prioritize it
- knows how to use a stronger engineer well

If every answer returns to backlog volume, capacity, or ticket pressure, assume the role may become ticket-driven.

### 7. End By Leaving A Clear Impression

The final impression should be simple:

You are reliable for delivery, but your unusual value is in technical shaping, design quality, and leverage across multiple workstreams.

He should leave the meeting thinking:

"This person is not just extra capacity. This person can improve how the team solves harder problems."

## Suggested Talking Points

You do not need to say all of these. Pick the ones that feel natural.

### Version 1: Balanced

> I am happy to deliver features, but I think the place where I create the most value is in areas where technical direction matters, especially problems that affect multiple projects or future delivery speed.

### Version 2: More Direct

> I am strongest when I can work on higher-leverage technical problems, not only implement predefined tasks. I would want to contribute through ownership of an area, not only ticket execution.

### Version 3: Linked To His Team

> I understand the team is busy with backlog. In that kind of environment, I think I can help most by addressing some of the structural issues behind the pressure, not only by taking more items from the queue.

### Version 4: Testing Openness

> If I joined your team, is that something you would want from me? Is there room for someone to take ownership of system-level improvements as part of normal delivery?

## What Not To Do

Avoid these mistakes:

- do not say you are above tickets
- do not imply his current team lacks thinking
- do not make the conversation about his weakness
- do not sound like you want freedom without accountability
- do not give a big abstract architecture lecture

Even if some of those thoughts are true, they are not useful in the meeting.

## How To Read The Outcome

Good outcome:

- he becomes curious about your thinking
- he asks what areas you would want to own
- he sees system design as a delivery enabler
- he gives examples of where you could shape direction
- he shows some mechanism for making room for that work

Bad outcome:

- he agrees politely but stays vague
- he praises your experience but only talks about execution load
- he has no model except backlog reduction
- he cannot describe how technical ownership would work in practice

If the outcome is weak, believe the signal. Friendly tone does not fix a structurally weak role.

## The Main Principle

In this reorg, you should not optimize only for where you are wanted.

You should optimize for where your value can still compound.

That means:

- work that interests you
- a role with real ownership
- a manager who can at least recognize and use technical leverage
- an environment where you are more than ticket throughput

The conversation is one step in answering that question.

Its purpose is not to win approval.

Its purpose is to see whether this team has room for the kind of engineer you want to keep being.
