# Bad Tech Leader Classification

## Core Idea

Not all bad tech leaders fail in the same way.

The most useful classification is not by personality, but by what is broken.

Three angles explain most cases:

- technical understanding
- respect for tech
- control style

## The Three Axes

### 1. Technical Understanding

This asks whether the leader can understand:

- system constraints
- engineering tradeoffs
- architecture consequences
- cost, reliability, and complexity

This does not mean they must be the strongest engineer in the room.

It means they need enough technical judgment to avoid careless decisions.

### 2. Respect For Tech

This is the minimum bar.

Even if a leader is not deep technically, they should still:

- respect technical reality
- respect experts who know more
- respect engineering time and complexity
- respect debt, reliability, and maintainability

Low technical depth can be survivable.

Low respect is much more dangerous.

### 3. Control Style

This asks how the leader behaves when work is happening.

At one end, they abdicate.

- they drop everything to the team
- provide no guidance
- provide no accountability
- disappear when hard tradeoffs appear

At the other end, they micromanage.

- they control implementation details
- override engineers without enough context
- create approval bottlenecks
- reduce team ownership

Healthy control is in the middle:

- clear direction
- real delegation
- support when needed
- accountability without suffocation

## A Simple Diagnostic Model

Most bad tech leaders can be placed somewhere in this three-axis space:

- low or high technical understanding
- low or high respect for tech
- abdication or micromanagement control style

The most dangerous combinations are:

1. Low tech + low respect + abdication

What it looks like:

- does not understand the work
- does not value technical reasoning
- throws problems to the team and walks away

Risk:

- neglected architecture
- hidden risks
- weak ownership
- blame later when things fail

2. Low tech + low respect + micromanagement

What it looks like:

- does not understand the work
- still interferes constantly
- pushes shallow opinions as commands

Risk:

- bad decisions with high confidence
- team frustration
- slow delivery
- quality damage

3. Medium tech + low learning + micromanagement

What it looks like:

- was once technically strong
- has not updated their mental model
- keeps controlling based on old assumptions

Risk:

- outdated standards
- blocking useful change
- false certainty
- loss of strong engineers

4. Low tech + high respect + healthy delegation

What it looks like:

- not ideal on technical depth
- but honest about limits
- relies on strong engineers appropriately
- helps the team make good decisions

Risk:

- still dependent on quality of technical staff

This type is imperfect, but can still function well.

## Your Two Examples

### Type A: Knows Little Tech And Drops Everything To Team Members

This is mainly:

- low technical understanding
- weak accountability
- abdication instead of delegation

Main failure:

The leader is not leading. They are only forwarding responsibility downward.

### Type B: Old Tech Leader, Does Not Know New Tech, But Micromanages

This is mainly:

- stale technical model
- weak learning ability
- micromanagement

Main failure:

The leader combines outdated knowledge with excessive control.

That is often worse than simply being weak technically.

## A Practical Rule

Low technical depth alone does not automatically make someone a bad tech leader.

The bigger problem is usually one of these:

- low respect for tech
- bad control style
- inability to learn
- inability to make reality-based tradeoffs

So the real minimum bar is:

enough technical understanding to avoid careless decisions, and enough humility to respect people who know more.

## Short Version

If you want a compact classification, use this:

1. Weak in tech
2. Weak in respect for tech
3. Wrong control style

Everything else is usually a variation or combination of these.
